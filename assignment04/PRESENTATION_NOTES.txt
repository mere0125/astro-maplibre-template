The Temporary Forest
Assignment 4 - two-minute presentation
Mere Cui

My project, The Temporary Forest, asks what becomes visible when vacant
land is read as a living surface rather than only an administrative
category. This website is an interactive field index for that larger
atlas. It moves from all five boroughs to an East New York pilot and
finally to individual lots.

At the city scale, the map starts with PLUTO records classified as vacant
land. The inventory contains 24,663 records, but only 24,213 have usable
geometry. Keeping those counts separate makes the coverage of the map
explicit.

Inside East New York, I return to the original 254-lot screening window.
Fourteen parcels have comparable historical observations and vegetation
measurements. Eight appear open in both 1996 and 2018. Six show a building
in 1996 and open ground in 2018. Their current vacant status comes from
the 2026 administrative record.

Selecting a lot lets the viewer compare dated aerial images and inspect
its 2022 vegetation response. The percentage measures image pixels with
NDVI above 0.20. It is a spectral indicator, not a measurement of forest
or habitat quality. Filters make the historical groups and vegetation
classes easier to compare.

The two groups have almost identical median responses, around 63 percent.
This small sample therefore does not support the assumption that older
vacancy automatically becomes greener.

I gave the interface the feeling of a quiet field notebook, with sage
colours, Times typography, botanical details, and parcel-shaped marks.
The interactions invite close observation while keeping uncertainty
visible. They show where the atlas needs more evidence, especially field
observations, rather than presenting every green lot as a temporary
forest.

Presentation sequence

1. Start at New York City and point out the distinction between records
   and mapped features.
2. Enter East New York and explain the 254-lot screening context and the
   fourteen analysed sites.
3. Select one parcel. Compare two dated images and read its NDVI result.
4. Use one filter to show that the interface supports exploration.
5. End with the similar group medians and the need for field observation.
